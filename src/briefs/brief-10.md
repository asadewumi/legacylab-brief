## One, the policy change

### Nigeria is putting the NIN at the boarding gate, and nobody has published what happens to a passenger who has not got one

Festus Keyamo, Minister of Aviation and Aerospace Development, unveiled the V-Pass passenger verification system at the domestic terminal of Murtala Muhammed International Airport in Lagos on Monday 16 November 2026. He directed the Nigeria Civil Aviation Authority to give every airline until the end of December to key into it, and said that from 1 January 2027 no airline will be allowed to board passengers if it has not. The requirement covers scheduled domestic flights. Rollout begins at Lagos and Abuja with the rest of the domestic airports to follow, and international identification is handled through a separate arrangement involving the Ministry of Interior.

What V-Pass does is tie boarding to the National Identification Number. Passengers register with their NIN details, the last four digits of the identification number open designated security gates, and verification at boarding runs on iris and facial recognition or on scanning the QR code on the boarding pass. The platform is linked to the NIN database. The Federal Airports Authority of Nigeria is in charge of the equipment, its Managing Director and Chief Executive Officer Olubunmi Kuku named VERXID Technologies Limited as its private sector partner, and Dr Jobson Ewalefoh, Director-General of the Infrastructure Concession Regulatory Commission, was present at the unveiling. Reporting gives two different expansions of the name, Airport Passenger Verification System and Virtual Passenger Processing System, which is a small sign of how little about this is written down.

Now the part that makes it item one. The deadline announced is the airline's, not the passenger's. The minister said airlines must key in, and the 1 January date is the date an airline that has not keyed in may not board anybody. Nothing published this week says what happens to a passenger who turns up without a NIN, and no reporting read for this item addresses it. No regulation, circular or Act is named anywhere either. The reporting mentions Federal Executive Council approval last year and vetting by the Attorney-General of the Federation, but neither is a published instrument you can read. On data protection, the only assurance on the record is the operator's own, with Daniel Bankole, Managing Director of VERXID Technologies Limited, saying the company's role includes protecting passenger data and complying with relevant data protection laws. No law is named, and nothing has been said about which passenger data is shared, with whom, or for how long it is kept. This is the second time this series has watched the NIN become the key to something, after Brief #04 on the digital postcode system and its planned link between building codes and National Identification Numbers.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> First, the thirty-second job. If you fly domestically, check now that you have a NIN and that the name on it matches the name you book tickets in, because a mismatch discovered at a gate in January is a missed flight and the enrolment queue is not a same-day errand. Do the same for anyone you book travel for. Second, the thing to watch rather than act on. A deadline that binds airlines does not by itself bar anybody from a plane, and no rule has been published that does. Treat the headlines saying no NIN means no flight as the outlets' framing rather than as the announced policy, and watch for the actual instrument, because that is the document that will say what happens at the gate and what the exceptions are. Third, if you build anything that touches identity. This is a national identity number becoming the key to a physical service, operated by a private company under a concession, with biometric capture at the point of use and no published data-sharing terms. That is a pattern worth learning to read, because it is spreading and the questions are always the same three: what is collected, who holds it, and what is the retention period. Brief #07 carried Interpol's finding that synthetic identities are being used across Africa to open bank accounts and register SIM cards, which is a fair argument for stronger verification at the gate and an equally fair argument for knowing exactly where the resulting database sits.</p>

## Two, the funding move

### Four billion naira is going to the organisations that train Nigerians, and applications close on 30 November

The Federal Ministry of Communications, Innovation and Digital Economy opened the 3MTT Delivery Network on 11 November 2026, with about four billion naira earmarked to support verified outcomes in Cohorts 5 and 6. The ministry says about, so the exact figure is not published. Funding applications for Cohort 5 close at 23:59 West Africa Time on Monday 30 November 2026, through delivery.3mtt.gov.ng. They can be made alone or as a consortium, and they must be led by an organisation registered and operating in Nigeria.

There are two doors, and they are not the same door. Membership of the Delivery Network is open to organisations and to individual experts, in Nigeria and abroad, with applications reviewed every quarter and no closing date. Accepted members become Enabled Delivery Partners and get access to the 3MTT Talent Operating System, the National Talent Registry, insight into the roles and skills employers are actually asking for, and access to device financing and partner resources subject to eligibility. The funding stream is the narrower one. Organisations selected for Cohort 5 become Funded Delivery Partners and train fellows against verified employer demand, and the financing is results-based, which the ministry describes as financing following verified results. Cohort 5 is funded by the European Union and Denmark through the GIZ Digital Transformation Center Nigeria, HAUS and ESTDEV, with a grant from the MacArthur Foundation through Blue Sapphire Hub. Dr Bosun Tijani, Honourable Minister of Communications, Innovation and Digital Economy, and Massimo De Luca, Head of Cooperation at the Delegation of the European Union to Nigeria and ECOWAS, both spoke to it.

The scale figures the ministry gives are worth holding together. More than 1.8 million Nigerians have registered on the 3MTT platform. 179,000 Fellows have been trained across four cohorts and the DeepTech programme. More than 200 partner organisations are involved across all 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory, and the programme is working towards a three million target. How many fellows Cohort 5 will train is not stated, and neither is an itemised list of what the money pays for. One note on what this item is. It is money for organisations that deliver training rather than a cheque to a learner or a round into a startup, so it puts no figure anywhere near the continental startup funding totals that Briefs #01, #03, #04, #06 and #08 took from trackers that count differently from each other.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> If your organisation trains anybody in anything digital, this is the most actionable thing in this Brief and you have ten days. Read the two routes carefully before you start, because they suit different people. The funding route needs a Nigerian-registered organisation to lead, so if you are an individual trainer or a small unregistered outfit, your realistic move is either the membership route, which stays open and is reviewed quarterly, or joining somebody else's consortium this week. The consortium option is the one most people overlook and it is explicitly allowed. Then read the words results-based twice. Financing that follows verified results means you are not paid for enrolling people, you are paid when something the programme counts as a result is confirmed, and that changes the shape of a budget completely. Find out what counts and what the verification lag is before you commit staff time, because a cash-flow assumption built on enrolment will hurt. The two numbers to take away even if you never apply are 1.8 million registered against 179,000 trained. That gap of roughly ten to one is the demand this money exists to convert, and it is also the most honest picture available of how much appetite for digital training there is in Nigeria against how much delivery capacity exists to meet it. If you are writing a funding proposal of your own to anybody at all, those two figures are a better opening than an adjective.</p>

## Three, the free course

### Elements of AI is written for the people who will never build a model but keep having to make decisions about one

Elements of AI is a free online introduction to artificial intelligence for non-experts, created by MinnaLearn, an online learning company, and the University of Helsinki, which its own pages describe as the oldest and largest institution of academic education in Finland. There are two courses. An Introduction to AI is the first, and the site states plainly that it needs no complicated maths and no programming. Building AI is the second, and it recommends some basic Python. Both are described as free, both combine theory with practical exercises, and both can be completed at your own pace.

There is no cohort, no selection and no application. The site says more than two million people have signed up, with graduates in over 170 countries, and it names no country restriction. That makes it the opposite shape to the two courses this Brief checked and rejected this week, both of which were real and both of which had already closed. The AWS AI and Machine Learning Scholars programme run with Udacity has filled every place in its challenge phase and its own timeline shows the application window closing in June. The NITDA and JICA IgniteHer bootcamp for Nigerian women founders, which is genuinely worth watching for its next cohort, closed applications on 18 November, which was yesterday.

Three things the site does not answer, so this Brief claims nothing about them. It does not publish an expected number of hours on its front page. It does not say whether the Building AI certificate carries a fee, and a help entry refers to payment methods for that certificate, so do not assume the certificate is free simply because the course is. And whether European credits are available, and on what terms, is not answered on the pages read. The sensible reading is that the learning is free and the paperwork may not be, which is a common and perfectly honest arrangement, but it is worth checking before you promise a certificate to an employer.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> Most free AI training is aimed at people who intend to build something. This one is aimed at everybody else, and everybody else is the larger and worse-served group. If you sit in meetings where somebody proposes using AI for recruitment, for customer support, for grading, for loan decisions or for deciding who gets a service, the skill you need is not Python. It is being able to tell what a model can and cannot know, why a system trained on last year's decisions repeats last year's prejudices, and what question to ask when a vendor says the model is 95 percent accurate. That is what the first course teaches, and it does it without pretending you will enjoy the maths. It is also the cheapest way to stop being managed by the word AI. A great deal of what is sold into Nigerian organisations at the moment is ordinary software with the word attached, and the price goes up accordingly. Someone who can ask what the training data was and what happens when the model is wrong will save their organisation more money in one procurement conversation than the course costs in time. Two practical notes. Do the first course and stop there unless you actually want to build things, because the second is a different commitment. And because nothing will chase you, put a finishing date in your calendar tonight and tell one colleague what it is.</p>

## Four, the tool

### Stirling PDF does the things you currently upload your bank statement to a stranger's website to do

Stirling PDF describes itself as a free, private PDF editor you can run on any infrastructure, and its headline is that your PDFs stay on your servers. The download page offers desktop applications for Windows, macOS and Linux, free for individuals in its own words, and self-hosting with Docker, with Helm on Kubernetes, or by running the Java file on an ordinary server. The site says it carries more than 60 tools for editing PDFs and reports more than 30 million downloads. The operations it names include reading scanned pages with optical character recognition, redacting sensitive details, compressing files for archiving, checking signatures, classifying documents by type and sensitivity, converting and summarising. The source is published on GitHub, under Stirling-Tools and Stirling-PDF.

Four things are worth saying plainly, because the page is more careful than the summaries of it. The words used are free for individuals and free for individual use, and the site does not state what an organisation pays. A pricing page and a commercial automated pipeline product exist alongside the free one, and this Brief did not read them, so nothing is claimed about their terms. The pages read do not name the licence, although the repository is public. And the site does not say outright that your files never leave your machine. What it says is that the editor runs on your machine and that the source is public so you can verify that rather than trust it, which is a more honest claim and a better one. Note also that the connector features are designed to send files out to places like SharePoint, Slack, Google Drive and Dropbox, so that is a different mode of use and a different set of questions.

This is the first tool in this series whose argument is about a habit rather than about a price. The habit is uploading documents to whichever free PDF website appears first in a search, in order to merge two files, compress something under a portal's two megabyte limit, convert a scan, or sign a form.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> Think about what actually goes through those websites. International passport data pages for a visa application. Bank statements for a loan or a landlord. A signed contract. A client's medical or financial records. A staff list with phone numbers. CVs sent to you in confidence. In most cases you have no idea who operates the site, where the file is stored, how long it is kept or whether it is used to train something, and the one thing you can be sure of is that you agreed to terms you did not read. Running the same operations on your own machine removes that question entirely rather than answering it. For Nigerian organisations there is a second reason, which is the two megabyte upload limit that guards nearly every government and university portal in the country. Compression is the single most common reason anybody touches one of these sites, and it is the operation with the least excuse for leaving your laptop. Practical advice. If you are not technical, take the desktop application and ignore everything on the site about pipelines, connectors and servers, because that is the commercial side and you do not need it. If you handle other people's documents as part of your work, this is worth the twenty minutes it takes to install, and it is worth telling your team to stop using the websites. And if your organisation is going to standardise on it, read the pricing page first, because free for individuals is the wording and it leaves organisations unaddressed.</p>

## Five, the report worth reading

### African networks cover 92 percent of people and 28 percent use them, and the GSMA has found a lever that moves the number

The GSMA launched its Digital Africa Index 2026 on 18 November 2026 at the GSMA Digital Africa Summit in Cape Town, drawing on data from 54 African countries. The headline is that mobile broadband networks now cover 92 percent of Africa's population while only 28 percent use mobile internet on their own devices, leaving what the GSMA calls a 64 percent usage gap. Angela Wamola, Head of Africa at the GSMA, describes that gap as people living within coverage who are not using mobile internet.

Before the figures go any further, one piece of bookkeeping, because this series has been here before and the numbers are not interchangeable. Briefs #02 and #03 took the argument that coverage is not use from the GSMA. Brief #08 put the International Telecommunication Union's version beside it, 97 percent of the world covered by at least a 3G signal and Africa at 42 percent internet use. The 28 percent in this report is not that 42 percent restated or corrected. The ITU was measuring internet use by any route, including a shared device or a fixed connection. The GSMA is measuring mobile internet used on a person's own device. They are different indicators and they answer different questions, so do not set one against the other, and if you quote either, say which one it is and what it counts.

What makes this report worth a fourth visit to the subject is that it stops describing the gap and names something that closes it. Only nine of the 54 countries assessed impose neither sector-specific taxes nor import duties on mobile handsets, and that is down from ten in 2024, so the position has got slightly worse rather than better. Against that, South Africa removed a 9 percent excise duty on smartphones priced under 2,500 rand in April 2025, and on the GSMA's own analysis monthly entry-level smartphone sales then rose by about 80 percent, from around 250,000 in March 2025 to around 460,000 in February 2026. That is as close to a controlled experiment as this subject gets.

On policy, the index reports that ten African countries now combine stronger digital development with more enabling policy and regulation, up from three in 2024. South Africa and Kenya recorded the highest Digital Nations and Society Index scores, at 69 and 65 out of 100. Egypt and Ghana made the largest gains on the Digital Policy and Regulatory Index since 2024. Nigeria is named as one of seven countries that have joined Kenya, Mauritius and South Africa in the index's leading group. No Nigerian score is published in the release, so nothing is claimed here about where exactly Nigeria sits or what it would take to move.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> The useful shift in this report is from describing a problem to pricing it. For years the answer to low usage has been framed as awareness, skills and content, all of which matter and none of which a government can do quickly. A handset duty is a line in a budget that can be removed in an afternoon, and South Africa has now provided a number for what happens when one is. Eighty percent more entry-level smartphones sold in a year is not a rounding error, and it is the first figure in this whole argument that a finance ministry rather than a digital ministry can act on. If you ever write to a legislator, respond to a consultation, or sit on a panel about digital inclusion in Nigeria, that is the single most useful fact you can carry, and it is checkable in the GSMA's own release rather than in a write-up. Two cautions. Do not take the Africa-specific figures circulating about this report from secondary coverage, because Brief #08 found exactly that going wrong with the ITU report, where write-ups carried African numbers that were not in the source. Go to the GSMA release. And hold the 28 percent against item one of this Brief. A country that is making the National Identification Number the key to boarding a plane, and that has already planned to link it to building postcodes, is building identity infrastructure on an assumption of personal device ownership that this index says does not yet hold for most adults on the continent. That is not an argument against doing it. It is an argument for asking, every time, what happens to the person who does not have the device.</p>
