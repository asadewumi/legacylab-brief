## One, the policy change

### The registry switched on, and nothing happened to your phone

We have written about the Nigerian Communications Commission device registry twice. Brief #02 said unregistered phones would not work on Nigerian networks. Brief #03 said the deadline for registering existing stock was Tuesday 6 October. The system is now running, and what it actually does is a good deal gentler than either of those sentences implied. That matters enough to say plainly.

The NCC briefed the press in Abuja on Tuesday 13 October, reported by Nairametrics the following day. Edoyemi Ogoh, Director of Technical Standards and Network Integrity at the NCC, said roughly 231 million devices already operating on Nigerian networks have been registered, and the commission did that automatically. Subscribers were not asked to do anything and do not need to do anything now. Devices that were already connected before the system became operational will not be disconnected and are not being blacklisted. The framework is called Device Checker and Type Approval Compliance, and it rests on the Type Approval Business Rules introduced in August 2024 and on section 132(2) of the Nigerian Communications Act 2003, which has required regulatory approval for telecom equipment since it was written.

The enforcement sits upstream rather than on your handset. The NCC is working with the Nigeria Customs Service, manufacturers, importers and dealers to authenticate devices before they reach the market, and the system is meant to show who imported a device, when, and in what quantity. Newly introduced devices have to meet the verification requirements before they can get on a network. If you want to check one, dial star three one five hash, or use the NCC Device Checker portal, and find your IMEI by dialling star hash zero six hash. If a device comes back unverified, the NCC's line is to go back to the seller for a replacement, and to take an unresolved complaint to the toll-free 315 helpline. Officials also said the system does not reach messages, photos, contacts or financial records, and works on device identification information only.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> If you own a phone, the short version is that you have nothing to do, and we would rather correct that than let two earlier Briefs stand. The real audience for this is anyone who imports, sells or repairs handsets, because the checkpoint has moved to the border and the invoice rather than to the subscriber. Verification now happens before stock reaches you, which means the risk of being left holding unsellable inventory has shifted onto whoever brought it in. Check your incoming stock through the Device Checker before you pay for it, not after, and keep the import paperwork where you can find it. One thing is still missing, and it has been missing since Brief #03. There is still no published appeal route for a handset flagged because its IMEI is legitimately damaged, and nothing in this briefing addressed it. Until that exists, a damaged IMEI is a commercial risk with no stated way out.</p>

## Two, the funding move

### An electric fleet reached 29 million dollars, and the useful number is not the money

MAX, the Lagos electric mobility company founded in 2015 by Adetayo Bamiduro and Chinedu Azodoh, has extended its Series B3 round to 29 million dollars. The new money comes from Nithio, a United States climate fintech, and the size of Nithio's own cheque was not disclosed. It was announced on 14 and 15 October. This is a single disclosed deal rather than a tracker total, so nothing here is comparable to the continental figures in Brief #01, Brief #03 or Brief #04.

What MAX actually sells is worth understanding, because it is not a vehicle. Commercial transport operators subscribe, and the subscription bundles the vehicle financing, access to battery-swapping stations, digital payments and fleet management software into one monthly relationship. The company says it operates in 20 cities across Nigeria, Ghana and Cameroon, has served more than 60,000 commercial transport operators, has facilitated over a billion trips, and estimates it has avoided around 238,000 tonnes of greenhouse gas emissions. It raised 24 million dollars in January 2026, and it is targeting 250,000 drivers and more than 150 million dollars in annual recurring revenue by 2027.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> Put the two numbers side by side. More than 60,000 operators now, a stated target of 250,000 by next year. That is roughly a fourfold increase in customers inside about fifteen months, and it is a far more interesting figure than the 29 million dollars, because it is the one that has to be true for the money to make sense. If you build anything that sells to Nigerian small operators, drivers, traders, artisans, repairers, the structure here is the lesson rather than the sector. MAX did not sell a product to people who could not afford one. It wrapped the thing, the financing, the fuel equivalent and the software into a single recurring payment sized to a daily income. That is the shape that clears the affordability problem, and it works in a good many businesses that have nothing to do with motorcycles. It is also worth noticing who wrote the cheque. A climate fintech funded this, not a mobility fund, which says the money available to Nigerian founders right now may be sitting under a label you have not thought to read.</p>

## Three, the free course

### A fully funded data and AI course, out of the University of Lagos

NitHub, the innovation and incubation hub at the University of Lagos, is running a digital skills programme with DataCamp, through DataCamp's donation arm. It is free, it is fully online and self-paced, and it is open to students, recent graduates, career changers and self-taught learners. The application form is hosted on a University of Lagos account and was open when we checked it on 15 October.

What you get is funded access to DataCamp's paid learning platform, built into structured learning paths with projects on real datasets, plus certifications and a learning community. The subject areas are data analysis, data science, machine learning, artificial intelligence, SQL, Python and data visualisation. You need a laptop, an internet connection and the time to use them, and the form asks for your category, your contact details and a short answer on why you want it.

Two honest gaps. The organisers say places are limited but have not published how many, and no closing date has been given, so the practical advice is to apply now rather than at the weekend. The programme also has not been announced in a NitHub or DataCamp press release that we could find, so treat the form as what it is, a University of Lagos application form, and give it the ordinary care you would give any form asking for your phone number.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> This one is a real offer rather than a three-hour badge, and the difference is the platform access. A DataCamp subscription is a genuine recurring dollar cost, and the learning paths are long enough to change what you can do rather than what your profile says. So the thing that decides whether this is worth anything is not the application, it is whether you book the hours. Pick one path, data analysis with SQL is the one that most reliably turns into paid work in Nigeria, and put four fixed hours a week in your calendar before you apply, not after you are accepted. Funded access that goes unused is the most common outcome of every scholarship like this, and it is not the fault of the scholarship.</p>

## Four, the tool

### A business stack built in Lagos, with a plan that stays free

startbuddi is a business operations platform built in Nigeria by Joanna Okedara-Kalu, inside Tech Della Solutions Limited, the company behind Techdella. It is live now. It bundles the things a small service business usually pays for separately into one workspace: a CRM and contact list, deal pipelines, a shared inbox, bookings, invoicing, forms, projects and tasks, documents, email marketing and team collaboration. There is also an AI assistant called Chip, which answers website visitors, handles follow-ups and has voice calls in beta.

The pricing is the part to read carefully, and we checked the product itself rather than the announcement. There is a permanent free plan and it does not ask for a card. On the free plan you can build and preview a client portal and start from a website template at no cost. Inviting clients into a portal needs the Starter plan, webinars need Starter, and calls inside a portal need Growth. Live call capacity runs at 10, 25 and 50 people on Starter, Growth and Scale. The company has not published the prices of those three tiers anywhere we could find, which is a fair thing to know before you build your operations on it.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> The reason to look at this is not the feature list, which reads like every other all-in-one platform. It is the pricing currency. A Nigerian service business running a foreign CRM, a foreign scheduler and a foreign email tool is paying three dollar subscriptions out of naira revenue, and that gap widens every time the exchange rate moves. A product built here is at least likely to be priced for here. Test it the cheap way. The free plan costs nothing and asks for no card, so spend an hour putting your ten most recent clients and your actual enquiry process into it, and see whether it survives contact with how you really work. Do that before the free plan matters to you, because the boundary that will decide it is where the free tier stops. Here that boundary is client access to portals, which is to say the moment it becomes useful to anyone but you. Find out what Starter costs before you move anything important.</p>

## Five, the report worth reading

### The World Bank says Africa's AI opening is small cheap tools, not big models

The World Bank published the latest edition of its Africa Economic Update on 6 October. It is the Bank's twice-yearly economic report for the region, and this edition puts artificial intelligence at the centre of it.

The macroeconomic picture first, because it frames everything else. Regional growth is projected to rise from 4.1 percent in 2025 to 4.3 percent in 2026, which is 0.3 percentage points above the Bank's own April forecast, and growth forecasts were revised upward for nearly three-quarters of countries in the region, Nigeria among them. Median inflation is projected to go the wrong way, from 3.7 percent in 2025 to 5.5 percent in 2026. Public debt has broadly stabilised at around 57 percent of regional GDP.

On AI, the finding worth carrying is a judgement rather than a number. The Bank's position is that most countries in the region are at an early stage of adoption, that activity is concentrated in a small number of economies, with Nigeria, Kenya and South Africa named, and that the region's greatest opportunity lies in affordable, locally adapted small AI applications, including low-bandwidth tools, rather than in frontier AI systems. BusinessDay's reporting of the same report on 8 October adds figures the Bank's own release does not carry: an emerging AI economy that could create 35 to 40 million digital jobs, roughly 44 percent of larger companies in Kenya and Nigeria already using AI against 61 percent in the United States, about 2.6 percent of Sub-Saharan African jobs at near-term automation risk against 15.2 percent that could be augmented, around 900 million Africans still offline, and about 5 percent of African data centres AI-ready.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> Low-bandwidth is the operative word in the whole report, and it is a product brief hiding inside an economic update. The applications that will work here are the ones that survive a bad connection, a cheap Android phone and an electricity cut, which rules out most of what is being built in San Francisco and almost none of what could be built in Lagos. That is a genuine local advantage and it is rarely described as one. If you are building, the constraint you have been apologising to clients about is the specification. Design for offline first, small payloads and a phone that is three generations old, and you are building for a market of hundreds of millions that the frontier labs are not serving. The augmentation figure is the one to hold if you are employed rather than building. Roughly six times more jobs in the region stand to be augmented than automated in the near term, so the useful question this year is not whether your job survives, it is which two hours of your week you can hand over and what you do with them.</p>
