## One, the policy change

### Nigeria now has an apex institution for digital literacy, and nobody has published the document that made it one

The Nigerian Academy of Education has been designated the country's apex national institution for digital literacy and for developing leaders for a future-ready economy. It was announced on 4 November at the opening of the Academy's own 2026 Annual Congress in Abuja, and the Minister of Education, members of the National and State Assemblies and the heads of education parastatals were in the room.

Here is the part worth being careful about, and it is the reason this is item one rather than a footnote. The announcement was made by Emeritus Professor Olugbemiro Jegede, President of the Nigerian Academy of Education, which is to say by the head of the body being designated. No gazette, circular, approval or memo has been published, none of the reporting names the official or the office that conferred the designation, and no effective date, funding line or reporting duty has been stated anywhere. Voice of Nigeria, the Vanguard and the Authority all carried it on or about 4 November, and all three attribute the act to the Federal Government without naming a document.

What the Academy says the role involves is leading the development of Nigeria's national digital skills framework and providing intellectual leadership on policies that prepare the future workforce, with artificial intelligence and emerging technologies worked into education policy and practice. Alongside that, Jegede called for an urgent review of the National Policy on Education, a restructuring of teacher education, and policies guaranteeing access to digital technology in classrooms regardless of location. Those are demands addressed to government, not things government has agreed to, and the reporting does not say anyone answered them. Professor Josiah Olusegun Ajiboye, the Academy's First Vice-President, was among four academics invested as Fellows at the same congress.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> If you work in training, edtech, curriculum or teacher development, a national digital skills framework is the document that will eventually decide what counts as a qualification, who can award one and what a school is obliged to teach. Knowing which body holds the pen is worth more than knowing the announcement happened. But hold it loosely, because an announcement made by the beneficiary at its own event, with no instrument behind it, is not the same as a mandate with a budget, and the history of Nigerian education policy is full of apex bodies that were named and never resourced. The practical test to watch for over the next few months is narrow and it will tell you everything: does a framework actually get drafted, and does the Federal Ministry of Education publish something with its own letterhead on it. Until one of those happens, treat this as a serious claim of authority rather than a settled fact, and if you are about to build a product or a proposal around it, ask for the designating document before you do. That is not scepticism about the Academy, which is a body of education scholars and a reasonable choice for the job. It is just the difference between a role and the paper that creates it.</p>

## Two, the funding move

### A Ghanaian company raised 1.8 million euros to trace shea, and women are 91 percent of the people it buys from

Vitara Agritech, which was previously called Sommalife, raised 1.8 million euros in early-stage equity funding, reported on 4 November. The round was led by the European Union-funded AgriFI facility, which is managed by EDFI Management Company, with the impact investor Acumen also taking part alongside private angel investors who were not named. The company was co-founded by Mawuse Gyisun. WeeTracker, which reported it, puts the amount at about 2 million dollars, and that dollar figure is the outlet's own conversion of the euro amount rather than a separately disclosed number, so the euro figure is the one to carry.

This is a single named deal with a named lead investor and it adds nothing to the continental funding totals that Briefs #01, #03, #04 and #06 drew from different trackers. It is not comparable to them and is not meant to be.

What the company does is the interesting part. It runs a platform called TreeSyt that tracks shea production, manages farmer networks and handles digital payments to collectors. The operational figures reported are 47,000 active women farmers engaged during the 2025 and 2026 harvest season, more than 150,000 producers registered on the platform, women making up 91 percent of its smallholder collectors, and household incomes said to have risen by about 20 percent. Those figures are stated by the outlet without a source being named for any of them, and the income figure is hedged in the reporting itself, so treat the 91 percent and the 20 percent as the company's account rather than as audited numbers.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> Strip out the shea and look at the shape, because the shape is the transferable part. This is a business whose actual product is a verifiable record of who grew something, who was paid for it and when. European buyers increasingly cannot sell an agricultural commodity without being able to show that chain, and the people holding the commodity at the start of it are smallholders with no paperwork. That gap between a regulatory requirement in one place and an absence of records in another is a durable commercial opening, and it exists in Nigerian cocoa, cashew, sesame, ginger and palm produce exactly as it does in Ghanaian shea. The second thing worth noticing is who the investor is. AgriFI is a development finance facility, not a venture fund chasing a ten-times return, which is why a company with this profile can raise at this stage at all. If you are building something with real unit economics and a slow, unglamorous growth curve, development finance and impact capital are a more honest match for you than the venture route everybody writes about, and the application processes are published rather than relationship-gated. Finally, the 91 percent is worth sitting with rather than skimming. A supply chain where nine collectors in ten are women is one where a payments record is also, quietly, the first formal financial history those women have ever had.</p>

## Three, the free course

### Google has put more than fifteen of its training programmes in one place, and the lessons keep working after the data runs out

Google has brought together more than fifteen of its existing training programmes on a single site called Skilling Africa with Google, at skillingafrica.withgoogle.com. Google's own page describes free courses in artificial intelligence, data, marketing and more, delivered as short lessons with a community around them. That wording is Google's. Almost everything else below comes from the reporting rather than from Google's own pages, and it is worth keeping the two apart.

According to MSME Africa, reporting on 3 November, and a separate write-up the same week, the catalogue pulls together Google Career Certificates in data analytics, IT support and cybersecurity, Google Cloud Skills for developers, the Hustle Academy small business training, and foundational artificial intelligence material. There is an AI study companion, reported variously as SCOUT or Scout, meant to answer questions and help organise a study schedule, and learners are said to earn verifiable badges on finishing a course. Languages at launch are reported as English, Swahili and Arabic, with Hausa and other African languages described as coming.

The feature that matters most in this region is the offline one. Alex Okosi, Managing Director for Africa at Google, is reported as saying learners can download lessons over wifi and then use the material offline without spending more on mobile data, and that the platform runs on entry-level devices without a separate app to install. Taiwo Kola-Ogunlade, Communications and Public Relations Manager for West Africa at Google, is named as making the announcement. The targets reported are three million people trained by 2030 and 250,000 graduates by the end of this year, with more than seven million young Africans said to have completed earlier Google digital training. No eligibility restriction, closing date or country list is published on Google's page or stated in the reporting, so nothing is claimed here about any of the three.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> The download-then-study-offline design is the whole point, and it is the first thing in months that directly answers the number in item five of this Brief. Nearly everyone in Nigeria has a signal. Far fewer can afford to keep a video streaming for forty minutes, and that is the gap that has quietly ended more online courses than any lack of motivation. If you have ever abandoned a course because the data ran out mid-lesson, the thing that defeated you was a design assumption, not a shortage of discipline on your part. Two pieces of practical advice. First, the Career Certificates inside this are the part with real labour-market recognition, so if you only have the attention for one track, start there rather than grazing. Second, and this is the honest caution, a consolidated catalogue of fifteen programmes is an invitation to start five things and finish none, and badges are cheap to collect and easy to accumulate without acquiring a skill anybody will pay for. Pick one certificate, download the whole first module tonight while you are on wifi, and treat the rest of the catalogue as something that will still be there in three months. Also worth saying plainly: Google's own site confirms the name, the address and that courses are free, and the rest of the detail above rests on reporting, so if a specific feature is the thing you are counting on, check it on the site before you commit.</p>

## Four, the tool

### The free version of DaVinci Resolve is the same application the professionals use, and almost nobody here has installed it

DaVinci Resolve 21, from Blackmagic Design, runs on Mac, Windows and Linux, and Blackmagic's own pages call the free version free without qualification. What that free version does is the surprising part. On Blackmagic's own description it edits and finishes at up to 60 frames a second at resolutions as high as Ultra HD, 3840 by 2160, and it carries extensive colour grading tools including luma, HSL and 3D keyers, a colour warper and HDR tools, plus video collage and elastic wave audio retiming. It also supports collaboration, so more than one person can work on the same project at the same time. Blackmagic's own line on its Studio page is that the free version is packed with more features than most paid software.

What is withheld is specific and worth knowing before you plan around it. DaVinci Resolve Studio, listed at 236 dollars and marked down from 295, is what adds up to 120 frames a second at up to 32K, real-time handling of professional 10-bit formats, hardware-accelerated H.264 and H.265 decoding and encoding, Dolby Vision and HDR10 plus grading, 45 extra effects including AI noise reduction and SuperScale upscaling, and the whole set of Neural Engine features, which is where magic mask, object removal, smart reframe, speed warp retiming and scene cut detection live. Immersive 3D audio and Dolby Atmos mastering, stereoscopic 3D, cinema package encoding, and Python and Lua scripting are Studio as well. The free version works with 8-bit formats.

Two things Blackmagic's pages do not say, so this Brief does not either. They do not state whether the Studio price is a one-off purchase or a subscription, and the marked-down price suggests a sale of unstated duration. And they say nothing about whether a watermark, an export limit or an account is involved in the free version, so check that on the download page yourself rather than taking it from here. Verified on Blackmagic Design's own product pages on 5 November 2026.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> This belongs in the Brief because of a specific mismatch. Across Nigeria there are a great many people editing video on cracked copies of other software, or paying a monthly subscription in dollars that has become genuinely painful at the current rate, and a free application that colour grades to broadcast standard is sitting there unused because it is assumed to be a crippled demo. It is not a demo. For almost all of the work a small studio, a church media team, a wedding videographer, a YouTube channel or a volunteer communications team actually does, the free version is the whole job. Three practical notes. It is a demanding application, so an older laptop with little memory will struggle, and that is the real barrier here rather than the price. The things you would genuinely miss are the Neural Engine conveniences, above all magic mask and object removal, which save hours rather than enabling anything impossible. And the learning curve is steep in a particular way, because Resolve was a colour grading tool first and an editor second, so the page layout assumes a professional post-production order of work. Budget a weekend for the interface, not an evening, and learn the editing and grading pages before you touch anything else. If you currently pay a monthly fee in foreign currency for something you use at this level, this is the clearest saving available to you this month.</p>

## Five, the report worth reading

### 6.3 billion people are online, 97 percent of the world has a signal, and Africa is at 42 percent

The International Telecommunication Union published Measuring digital development, Facts and Figures 2026 on 3 November, from Geneva. It is the United Nations agency for telecommunications and this is its annual connectivity assessment, so the figures below are the primary source rather than a write-up of one. Doreen Bogdan-Martin, ITU Secretary-General, and Cosmas Luckyson Zavazava, Director of the ITU Telecommunication Development Bureau, are the official voices on it.

The headline is that 6.3 billion people, or 76 percent of the world, now use the internet, up from a revised 6.1 billion a year ago, and that 2 billion remain unconnected. Africa is at 42 percent. For comparison the ITU puts the Commonwealth of Independent States, Europe and the Americas between 87 and 93 percent, Asia-Pacific at 80 percent and the Arab States at 74 percent. By income the split is starker than by region: 93 percent in high-income countries against 32 percent in low-income ones, with the least developed countries at 41 percent.

Then the figure that reframes all of the above. Some 97 percent of the world is covered by at least a 3G signal. Coverage is very nearly universal and use is not, which means the binding constraint is no longer whether a network reaches you. On the ITU's own numbers it is cost and devices. The median price of a data-only mobile broadband package is 1.2 percent of income per head globally and 6.1 percent in the least developed countries. Mobile phone ownership runs above 95 percent in high-income countries and at 57 percent in low-income ones. And 5G, where the next decade of services will be built, reaches 86 percent of people in high-income countries and 4 percent in low-income ones. The other gaps the ITU records are narrower but persistent: 79 percent of men against 74 percent of women, 86 percent of 15 to 24 year olds against 74 percent of everyone else, and 82 percent of urban residents against 63 percent of rural ones.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> This Brief has carried the coverage-is-not-use argument twice before, in Brief #02 on Rwanda and Brief #03 on the capability gap, both times from the GSMA. Here is the United Nations telecoms agency putting the same thing beyond argument with a single pair of numbers: 97 percent covered, 42 percent of Africans using it. Anyone still proposing to close the digital divide by building more towers is solving the 3 percent problem. The 55 percent problem is the price of a megabyte and the price of a handset. For anyone building a product here, this is a design brief rather than a statistic. It says your application has to survive a cheap Android phone, an intermittent connection and a user counting the cost of every megabyte, and it explains precisely why the Google courses in item three bothered to work offline. If you are writing a funding proposal, grant application or policy submission in the next few months, these are the numbers to cite, because they are a primary United Nations source published this week and they will hold for a year. One caution worth carrying: the ITU's release gives Africa one figure and no country breakdown, so nobody should read a Nigerian number out of it. Various write-ups of this report are circulating with Africa-specific figures on phone ownership and 5G that do not appear in the ITU's own release, and at least one has a ratio that contradicts the ITU's own numbers, so take your figures from the source.</p>
