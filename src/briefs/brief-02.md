## One, the policy change

### Every phone in Nigeria is about to need papers

The Nigerian Communications Commission has begun enforcing its Device Management System, a central registry that ties every mobile phone sold or activated in the country to its IMEI number. Licensed importers must hold NCC type approval, upload IMEI numbers and purchase invoices to the DMS portal, pay a validation fee for each device, and obtain a pre-authorisation QR code that the Nigeria Customs Service checks before a phone is whitelisted.

The fee is 670 naira for budget devices and 3,700 naira for high-end ones. Phones that are not registered will not work on Nigerian networks. The legal footing is the Type Approval Business Rules of August 2024 and the Nigerian Communications Act 2003, and the NCC has phased the rollout after consultations exposed implementation gaps, starting with existing inventory and then future imports.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> The stated aim is to shut out stolen, cloned and substandard handsets, which is a real problem worth solving. The risk sits in the pass-through. Validation fees and compliance overhead land on importers working on thin margins, and margins that thin usually end up in the shelf price. In a market where the cost of the handset is the main thing standing between a person and the internet, a policy that nudges phone prices upward is a connectivity policy whether it means to be or not. Hold that thought until item five.</p>

## Two, the funding move

### Nigerian security gets funded as a category of its own

Aeon, a Nigerian cybersecurity startup, has raised a 1 million dollar pre-seed round led by Terra Industries, a defence technology startup, with Resilience 17, Kaleo, DFS Labs, Ajim Capital and Seedstars participating.

Aeon is building a platform for organisations running critical digital infrastructure, aimed at a problem its chief executive Samuel Ogbonyomi describes plainly, operators with a dozen security tools and no single view of their exposure. He founded the company with Ben Eluan and Alex Idowu. The money goes into product, business development, and turning existing pilots with financial services and cloud infrastructure companies into paying deployments.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> One million dollars is a small round and that is not the point. The point is who wrote the cheque and what for. Security in Nigeria has mostly been funded as a feature inside a fintech, and here it is funded as a business in its own right, led by a defence technology investor. When a category starts attracting specialist capital rather than incidental capital, it usually means the buyers have started paying. If you work anywhere near infrastructure, that is a hiring signal as much as an investment one.</p>

## Three, the free course

### 32,000 funded places, and the queue is still moving

The Digital Training Academy is a six-month programme from the Federal Ministry of Education, delivered with the National Open University of Nigeria and Yaba College of Technology, built on 32,000 free Coursera learning licences. Tracks include software development, cybersecurity, AI and machine learning, cloud computing, IT automation, product management, fintech, natural language processing and quantum computing, with certifications from providers including Google, IBM and Huawei. There are no tuition fees.

The honest status. The portal opened on 17 August. 72,850 people applied, 19,699 met the entry requirements, and 16,000 were taken into the first intake, which leaves half the licences still to allocate. Registration runs as a rolling queue rather than a single deadline. Create an account with your National Identification Number and an active phone number, complete your background, choose a track, and sit a pre-assessment that either admits you, places you in the queue, or finds you ineligible. Open to Nigerian citizens aged 18 and above, including students, job seekers and people already working.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> Free training schemes usually fail on one of two things, the quality of the content or the follow-through. This one buys in content that is already proven and spends its own effort on access, which is the right way round. If you have been meaning to put a certification behind what you already do in practice, the cost here is your time and a pre-assessment.</p>

## Four, the tool

### KUMii, a matching platform that arrived with money attached

22 On Sloane launched KUMii on 16 September at GEC+Africa in Cape Town. It is a free platform that profiles African entrepreneurs and then matches them to funders, tender and procurement opportunities, mentors, learning material and business software. More than 4,000 startups and small businesses had registered by launch day.

The detail that makes it worth your time sits next to it rather than inside it. 22 On Sloane is raising 1 billion rand, roughly 63 million dollars, through Sloane Capital, to invest in startups and small and medium enterprises across the continent.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> Directories of opportunity are the most common thing in African startup infrastructure and the least useful, because almost all of them are one-sided. A platform that lists funders without having any is a search engine with extra steps. This one launched alongside the capital it intends to deploy, which is the part that is normally missing. Treat it the way you would treat any new front door. Build the profile properly, because a matching engine can only match what you gave it, and keep your own pipeline running regardless.</p>

## Five, the report worth reading

### Coverage is not the problem

The GSMA published Rwanda Digital Economy, Policy reforms that will drive Rwanda's digital future, on 17 September.

The finding that should travel is this. Rwanda has 4G coverage over 96 percent of its population and 3G over 99 percent, and mobile internet penetration of 21 percent. Almost everybody can get online. Roughly one in five does. The report puts the gap down chiefly to what a smartphone costs, and secondarily to digital skills and the shortage of services in Kinyarwanda.

Its four recommendations are to make smartphones more affordable, strengthen digital skills training, expand local-language services and support sustainable investment in connectivity. GSMA estimates that acting on them would put more than a million additional Rwandans online by 2031 and produce a net fiscal benefit of 218 billion Rwandan francs a year.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> Read it as a template, not a country study. The distance between coverage and use is the defining number in most African markets, Nigeria included, and it does not close by building more towers. It closes on price, on skills, and on whether there is anything worth doing online in a language you think in. Now read it against item one, where a well-intentioned registry adds cost to the handset. Two sensible policies, pulling in opposite directions, in the same fortnight.</p>
