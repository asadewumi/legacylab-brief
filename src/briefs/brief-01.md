## One, the policy change

### Nigeria now has a national cloud rulebook

On 14 September, NITDA released four documents at once. The National Cloud Computing Guideline, the National Cloud Technical Guideline, the National Digital Infrastructure Assurance Framework, and the National Cloud Investment Strategy.

Strip away the titles and three things actually changed. Government institutions now operate under a Cloud First mandate, which turns the public sector into a guaranteed customer rather than a reluctant one. A single national certification, NDIAF, replaces the scatter of separate assessments providers used to chase. And approvals that were previously sought one by one from NITDA, the CBN, the NCC, NIPC and the NDPC now run through unified entry points.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> If you build anything that stores Nigerian data, the compliance path just became legible. The domestic cloud market is projected to move from 1.03 billion dollars in 2025 to 3.28 billion by 2030. That growth gets served by whoever is certified when the demand arrives, not by whoever starts the paperwork afterwards.</p>

## Two, the funding move

### Nigeria is back on top, and August is the reason

African startups raised 2.10 billion dollars across 275 deals between January and August. That is barely up on the same window last year, a 1.4 percent increase, so the headline number is flat.

The interesting part sits underneath it. Nigeria leads the continent with 528.6 million dollars, ahead of Benin, Egypt, South Africa and Kenya. And August alone brought in 438 million, a 368 percent jump on August last year. Moove took 250 million of that in a single Series C.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> A flat year with one enormous month is not a recovery, it is concentration. Capital is moving towards fewer, larger, later-stage companies. If you are raising early, the room has not got easier. It has got more selective.</p>

## Three, the free course

### MIT-level AI training, at no cost, for Nigerians

3MTT has opened a three-month AI programme built with MIT Open Learning. It covers machine learning fundamentals, deep learning, generative AI and large language models, and how all of it gets applied to real problems.

It is free. Not free-trial free. Free. It runs self-paced online, and it is open to any Nigerian aged 18 or over who is a 3MTT Fellow or is willing to become one.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> Selection is merit-based and slots are limited, which in practice means the people who apply in week one have better odds than the people who apply in week eight. If you have been meaning to get serious about AI and the fee was the obstacle, the obstacle is gone.</p>

## Four, the tool

### Penpot

Penpot is a design and prototyping tool that runs in your browser. It does what the expensive one does. Interface design, clickable prototypes, shared design systems, clean handoff to developers.

The free plan is not a trial and it is not a crippled version. Fully featured, 10GB of storage, up to eight people on a team, and unlimited viewers on top of that. It is open source, so your files stay yours and nothing sits hostage behind a renewal date.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> For a small studio or a two-person product team in Nigeria, per-seat design software priced in dollars is often the hardest subscription to justify. This takes that line out of the budget entirely.</p>

## Five, the report worth reading

### Africa Youth Employment Outlook 2026

Produced by the World Data Lab with the Mastercard Foundation and the University of Cape Town.

Four numbers carry the whole document. Africa has 532 million people aged 15 to 35, more than a fifth of that age group worldwide. Around 57 percent of them are working. Ninety percent of those jobs are informal. And only 9 percent of young Africans have completed tertiary education.

Read those together and the familiar framing falls apart. The problem is not that young Africans are not working. Most of them are. The problem is that the work is informal, unprotected and often does not pay enough to leave poverty, and that the credential route out of it is closed to more than nine in ten.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> It is the clearest argument yet for skills that can be demonstrated rather than certified. The report also projects services overtaking agriculture as the largest employer of young Africans by 2033. That is the direction the ground is moving.</p>
