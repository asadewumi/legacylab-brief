## One, the policy change

### Every government website is about to be graded on something most Nigerian businesses have switched off

If you run any website at all, the acronym to learn this week is DNSSEC, because the Nigerian government has just made it a thing agencies get marked on. The National Information Technology Development Agency has added DNS Security Extensions to the cybersecurity assessment criteria it applies to every Ministry, Department and Agency, and has called on government sites running on the .gov.ng domain to switch it on. Kashifu Inuwa Abdullahi, Director General of the National Information Technology Development Agency, disclosed it, with the address delivered on his behalf by Oluwasegun Falayan. No compliance deadline has been published, which is the one weak point in the announcement.

It was announced at a cybersecurity awareness webinar run by the Nigeria Internet Registration Association through its .ng Academy, during Cybersecurity Awareness Month, under the theme Securing Nigeria's Digital Foundation, DNSSEC, the .ng Domain, and the Future of National Cyber Resilience. Adesola Akinsanya, President of the Nigeria Internet Registration Association, told the session that the registry switched DNSSEC on for the .ng domain itself in April this year, so the foundation the agencies are being asked to build on already exists.

Two figures were put beside it. Citing Check Point, Akinsanya said the average Nigerian organisation faced about 4,361 attempted cyberattacks a week in June, against a global average of roughly 2,270. And the Nigeria Inter-Bank Settlement System puts digital payment fraud at 25.85 billion naira in 2025, even though the number of reported incidents fell. Daniel Onyekpeze, Head of Incident Handling at the Nigeria Computer Emergency Response Team, went further and proposed an Executive Order giving DNSSEC Critical National Information Infrastructure status. That is a proposal made in the room, not a measure anyone has adopted, and it is worth keeping those two apart.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> Here is the plain version of what DNSSEC does, because the acronym hides something simple. The Domain Name System is the phone book that turns a name you typed into the address of a machine. Nothing in the original design proves the answer came from the real owner of that name, so an answer can be forged, and you can end up on somebody else's server with the right name still showing in your address bar. DNSSEC signs the answers so your device can check they are genuine. It does not protect the content of your site and it is not a firewall. It protects the act of arriving at you. The practical move is small and most people have never made it. Ask whoever runs your domain, your registrar or your hosting company, whether DNSSEC is enabled on it, because on most of them it is a single setting and costs nothing to turn on. If you build or audit systems for government, this is now something you will be assessed against, which also makes it billable work, and the absence of a published deadline means the sensible assumption is the next assessment cycle rather than next year.</p>

## Two, the funding move

### Andela's co-founder is back, and this time the product is AI engineers

Talent Nation announced a pre-seed round on 28 October. The amount was not disclosed, and nobody covering it has the figure, so treat this as a named deal with an unnamed number rather than something you can put in a table. It does not compare with the funding trackers used in Brief #01, Brief #03, Brief #04 or Brief #06, and it is not meant to.

The round was co-led by Enza Capital and the London firm LocalGlobe, with Future Africa, Kaleo Ventures, Ajim Capital, Solving.org and Accelerate Africa also taking part. Iyinoluwa Aboyeji, who co-founded Andela and Flutterwave, is executive chairman. Kesiah Valkin, who is South African-born and co-founded the London coding school 01 Founders, is chief executive. The company trains, certifies and places AI engineers from Africa with businesses anywhere in the world, and says it intends to train and deploy one million of them over the next decade.

The numbers it gives for the work so far are the interesting part. More than 63,000 applications in eighteen months. More than 32,000 candidates assessed. About 1,400 fellows so far, across nine campuses in six cities, through a training academy called Learn2Earn. Fewer than 3 percent of applicants are admitted. Candidates pay nothing upfront, and the company says enterprise revenue funds new engineers while graduates contribute towards later cohorts. It names IHS Towers among its clients. Sir John Lazar, a general partner at Enza Capital, framed the bet as the founders' ability to spot talent while holding to global standards, and Aboyeji's own line was that talent is the final constraint to super intelligence.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> Read the 3 percent before you read the million. On its own figures this is a selection business before it is a training business: sixty-three thousand people applied, about fourteen hundred are fellows, and the filter is doing most of the work. So the honest advice is not to treat this as the route in, because for roughly ninety-seven applicants in a hundred it will not be. What it is genuinely useful for is as a price signal. Serious money has just been put behind the proposition that the scarce thing in AI is not models or compute but people who can build with them to a standard a foreign enterprise will pay for, and that is a clear instruction about what to become. The second thing to sit with is older than this company. Aboyeji's first venture trained Nigerian engineers and sold their work abroad, and the standing criticism of that model was that it built careers for individuals and capacity for somebody else's economy. This one is explicitly global placement from the first sentence. That can be the best deal available to the engineer and still leave the question of who ends up with the capability, and if you are weighing a programme like this, those are two separate questions and both are yours to answer.</p>

## Three, the free course

### Fortinet has made its whole security curriculum free, with no deadline and no application

Last week's course had prerequisites, a cohort and a closing date. This one has none of the three. The Fortinet Training Institute's self-paced training catalogue is free, in the company's own words on its own page, which reads develop practical cybersecurity skills with free self-paced training from the Fortinet Training Institute. There is no application, no eligibility restriction stated anywhere on the page and no closing date, so it is available tonight and will still be available when you finish the thing you are already doing.

The structure runs from a Foundations level, NSE 1 to NSE 3, through NSE 4 on FortiOS, into NSE 5 to NSE 7 tracks covering secure networking, SASE, cloud security and security operations, up to NSE 8 at expert level. Alongside those sit industry certifications in MSSP Security Administration, MSSP Security Architecture and OT Security Architecture. You start at training.fortinet.com and work down the course index.

Two honest notes, because free training and free certification are not the same thing. The on-demand labs are sold separately, described on the page as available for purchase, and they are the part that builds the hands-on skill the courses describe. And the page does not publish what the certification exams cost, so plan for the training being free and the paper not necessarily being so. Verified on Fortinet's own page on 29 October 2026.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> The reason to take this seriously is in the first item of this Brief. Nigerian organisations are being probed roughly twice as often as the global average, government agencies are now being assessed on their security posture, and the people who can do that assessment work are in short supply. Vendor training is an unfashionable answer to that and a quietly effective one, because hiring managers advertise for named products rather than for concepts, and the certification names in this catalogue appear in real Nigerian job adverts. Be clear-eyed about the limit. A vendor curriculum teaches you one company's view of the world, so it is a strong first qualification and a poor only one. The combination that works is this for the employable specifics, paired with something vendor-neutral for the principles underneath. And the thing that makes this better than most free courses is the thing that makes it easy to postpone. There is no deadline, so nothing will ever force you to start. Put the first Foundations course in your calendar this week or accept that you are not going to do it.</p>

## Four, the tool

### The cheapest security upgrade available to you costs nothing and takes an evening

Bitwarden is a password manager, and the reason it belongs here rather than in a list of nice ideas is that its free plan is not a trial. The page describes it as basic password management, always free, and the free plan carries unlimited passwords and unlimited devices, with browser, mobile and desktop applications, free sharing with one other person, encrypted export of everything you have stored, and Bitwarden Send for passing something sensitive to somebody once. The software is open source.

What sits behind the paywall is worth knowing so you are not surprised. The built-in authenticator for one-time codes, file attachments, emergency access, and the vault health reports that tell you which of your passwords are reused or exposed, are all Premium, which is 19.80 dollars a year. A Families plan covering up to six people is 47.88 dollars a year. Verified on Bitwarden's own pricing page on 29 October 2026.

The one caveat is structural rather than commercial. Putting every password behind a single master password concentrates your whole risk in that one phrase, which means it has to be long, has to be unique, and must never have been used anywhere else. Written down on paper and kept somewhere only you can reach is a better place for it than reused from another account.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> Set this beside the Interpol finding further down. The attacks that are growing fastest in this region do not break encryption, they reuse a password that leaked somewhere else, or they persuade a person. One password used across your email, your bank and your business tools means the weakest of those three decides the security of all three, and you cannot fix that by trying harder to remember a better one. If you do nothing else after reading this, do it in this order. Your email first, because whoever controls it can reset everything else. Then whatever touches money. Then the account holding your business records. Three unique passwords, generated rather than invented, and the rest can follow over a month. For anyone running a team on no budget, which is most people reading this, note that free sharing with one other person is enough for a two-person business to stop sending credentials to each other on WhatsApp, which is the habit that quietly undoes everything else.</p>

## Five, the report worth reading

### Interpol says AI is now involved in more than half of Africa's reported cybercrime

Interpol published its African Cyberthreat Assessment Report 2026 on 3 August. It is forty pages, built on survey data from 36 African member countries, and its headline finding is the one worth carrying into next year: artificial intelligence is linked to 55 percent of reported cybercrimes across Africa. Neal Jetton, Director of Interpol's Cybercrime unit, is the official voice on it.

The scale numbers move in the wrong direction. The release reports losses rising from 192 million dollars to 484 million. Seventy-two percent of the countries surveyed reported scam centres operating on their territory, concentrated in Southern and West Africa. More than 1.1 billion mobile subscribers were recorded across the continent in 2025, which is the surface all of this runs on. TrendAI recorded some 600,000 sextortion detections. Against that, 17 countries enacted or amended cybercrime legislation in 2025, and four coordinated operations, Serengeti 2.0, Contender 3.0, Sentinel and Red Card 2.0, produced more than 1,500 arrests, hundreds of seized devices and the recovery of over 100 million dollars.

The regional pattern is specific rather than general. East Africa is described as a hub for mobile money fraud and for ransomware aimed at infrastructure. Central and West Africa are named for business email compromise and romance scams. Southern Africa's high connectivity is what draws global operators to it. The AI-enabled threats named are deepfake-driven sextortion and harassment, business email compromise messages that read convincingly, and synthetic identities used to open bank accounts, obtain mobile loans and register SIM cards. One thing to be accurate about: Interpol's own announcement names West Africa rather than Nigeria, and does not publish a country breakdown, so nobody should read a Nigerian figure out of it.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> The synthetic identity line is the one that should stop you. An invented person, assembled well enough to open a bank account, take a mobile loan and register a SIM, is a different problem from a stolen password, and it is the point where this report touches every identity and registration scheme Nigeria has been building. It also quietly explains why the two free things in this Brief are in it. If more than half of reported cybercrime now has AI somewhere in it, the attacks arriving at you are cheaper to produce, better written and more numerous than the ones you learned to spot, and the email with the odd grammar is no longer the tell. The defences that still work are unglamorous and almost free. A second channel to confirm any instruction involving money, used every single time and especially when the message sounds urgent and comes from somebody senior, because that is exactly what business email compromise is built to imitate. Unique passwords, so one leak stays one leak. And a habit of treating a voice or a face on a screen as a claim rather than proof, which five years ago would have been paranoid and this year is just literacy.</p>
