## One, the policy change

### The Senate has voted to let the central bank designate the biggest fintechs as systemically important, and published no threshold for it

The Senate passed SB. 959 on Tuesday 10 November, a bill to amend the Banks and Other Financial Institutions Act, No. 5 of 2020, to provide for the designation, registration and enhanced supervision of systemically important institutions. It went through clause-by-clause consideration at the Committee of the Whole and was passed for third reading, with Senate President Godswill Akpabio presiding. Senator Adetokunbo Abiru, who represents Lagos East and chairs the Senate Committee on Banking, Insurance and Other Financial Institutions, presented the committee's report, and two of the three outlets read for this item name him as the bill's sponsor alongside members of his committee. The bill had its second reading on Thursday 4 December 2025 and a public hearing on 4 March 2026, so this has taken eleven months to reach this point.

What it would do is give the Central Bank of Nigeria the power to identify and designate qualifying non-bank financial institutions, and fintech operators, as Systemically Important Institutions, and to put them under enhanced prudential supervision. There would be an official register of the ones designated. The criteria named are size, transaction volumes, customer base and operational interconnectedness with the financial system. Designated institutions would face stricter corporate governance, cybersecurity safeguards, technology-risk management and operational resilience requirements, stress testing, data-integrity assessments, enhanced regulatory reporting and supervisory access to their information and operational systems, strengthened beneficial ownership disclosure, and enforcement mechanisms with sanctions for breaches.

Here is what is not in any of it, and it is the reason this is item one. No numeric threshold is published, so nothing tells a company whether it is in scope. No capital requirement, licensing procedure, resolution regime, sanction amount or commencement date is stated anywhere in the reporting. And one chamber has acted: none of the three outlets records a vote in the House of Representatives or presidential assent, and all three describe it as a proposed law. It is not law. The Central Bank, the Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission, the Nigeria Deposit Insurance Corporation, the Nigerian Communications Commission, Ministry of Finance Incorporated, the Federal Ministry of Industry, Trade and Investment, the Bank of Industry and the Economic and Financial Crimes Commission all backed it at the public hearing, along with several banks. The Competition and Consumer Protection Commission asked for a clear line between prudential regulation, competition enforcement and consumer protection, and for coordination with the Central Bank to avoid duplicated regulation, and the reporting does not say that was settled. Verified across ThisDay, the Vanguard and The Whistler, all of 10 November 2026. This is the second item in this series on the Central Bank, after the payments data localisation directive in Brief #06, and it is a different instrument through a different route.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> If you run, work in or depend on a Nigerian payments, lending or wallet business, this is about where the regulatory perimeter is drawn, and the honest answer today is that nobody outside the Central Bank knows. Size, transaction volume, customer base and interconnectedness are four directions, not four thresholds, so you cannot work out whether you are in scope and should not pretend otherwise. What you can do is read the list of obligations, because it is a reasonable description of what a supervisor will eventually ask any sizeable operator for, whether or not this particular bill becomes law: documented technology-risk management, tested operational resilience, a clean record of who actually owns the company, and systems a regulator can be given access to. None of that is built in a quarter. If you are at any scale, the beneficial ownership records and the incident and resilience documentation are the two to start on now, because they are slow and they are useful regardless. The caution runs the other way too. A bill passed by one chamber after eleven months is not a deadline, and there is no commencement date to plan against, so do not let anybody sell you a compliance programme this week on the strength of it. Ask for the designation criteria when they are published, because that single document decides who this is actually about. And hold item five of this Brief alongside it, because the market being brought under supervision is one where, on the numbers reported this week, roughly one adult in eight has ever used digital credit at all.</p>

## Two, the funding move

### Helium Health won Google.org funding for maternal health AI, and the figure being quoted for it is nobody's figure

Helium Health, the Lagos health-technology company, announced on 4 November 2026 that it has received funding from Google.org after being selected as one of fifteen global recipients in the 30 million dollar Google.org Impact Challenge: AI for Government Innovation, with a place in a Google.org Accelerator that brings support from Google engineers and AI product experts.

The amount is the part to be careful about. Helium Health's own announcement does not say how much it received. The 2.5 million dollar figure now circulating comes from a startup deal tracker of 10 November 2026, not from Helium Health and not from Google, and the 30 million dollars is the programme total across all fifteen recipients rather than anybody's individual award. No Google announcement of this award could be found on Google's own blog or on google.org, and Google's side of it appears only as a quotation from Maggie Johnson, Global Head of Google.org, inside Helium Health's own post. So the honest position is that the award is confirmed and the amount is undisclosed. On the register of what this series has printed, that makes this a named award with an undisclosed amount, which adds no figure to the continental funding totals drawn from different trackers in Briefs #01, #03, #04, #06 and #08, and it is the first grant rather than an investment to take this slot.

What the money is for is well documented. The company is deploying clinical AI models into primary maternal healthcare through a product called HeliumMum, which uses AI to help pregnant women identify pregnancy risks early. The rollout spans Lagos, Delta, Kano and Oyo States, with Lagos as the primary operational launchpad, and the Lagos State Governor, Mr Babajide Olusola Sanwo-Olu, was briefed on it. He is given no funding role in the announcement. Dimeji Sofowora, CEO of Helium Health, says the company has already reached over 3.5 million women, and the stated goal is to reach an additional 1.9 million women over three years and contribute to a 30 percent reduction in maternal mortality. That 30 percent is a contribution the company hopes to make, not a result it has produced, and the announcement frames it that way. Dr Shona Oluwatola, Head of Public Health at Helium Health, says Nigeria accounts for nearly three in every ten maternal deaths globally.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> Take the amount problem first, because it will happen to you. A deal tracker publishes a number, three newsletters repeat it, and within a fortnight it is a fact that nobody ever sourced. If you are citing this award in a pitch, a proposal or a board paper, cite what the company and the funder actually say, which is that Helium Health is one of fifteen recipients in a 30 million dollar programme. That sentence is true and checkable. A per-company figure is neither. The same discipline is worth applying to your own announcements: if you do not disclose an amount, somebody else will estimate one and you will spend a year correcting it. Now the substance, which is the more interesting half. This is clinical AI going into primary care across four state health systems, which makes the buyer a government rather than a consumer, and that changes everything about the work. The model is not the hard part. The hard part is integrating with whatever records a primary health centre actually keeps, training staff who were not consulted, and surviving a change of commissioner. If you are building anything intended to run inside Nigerian public services, the lesson in this award is that the funders who pay for that work are philanthropic and development funders rather than venture investors, because the returns are slow and the customer is a ministry. Those funders publish their application processes, which is worth knowing if you have been waiting to be introduced to somebody.</p>

## Three, the free course

### IBM's free technology training needs no application, and the fully funded scholarship somebody sent you is not IBM's

IBM SkillsBuild, at skillsbuild.org, is IBM's own free learning catalogue. Its own pages say it offers 100 percent free learning, describe the adult learners route as 100 percent free and online, and advertise free artificial intelligence and technology learning in more than twenty languages. The subjects are artificial intelligence, cybersecurity, data, cloud, industry tools and professional skills, organised into learning plans, with digital credentials described on IBM's pages as industry-recognised. You sign up with a form at skillsbuild.org and start.

Checked on IBM's own pages on 12 November 2026, there is no eligibility restriction published at all. No age, no nationality, no residency, no education requirement, no employment condition. There is no application deadline and no cohort. Three free live events are listed for 23, 25 and 30 November 2026, and those are events rather than courses.

Which brings up the reason this is in the Brief. Several Nigerian opportunity sites are circulating this as the IBM SkillsBuild Fully Funded Digital Skills Scholarship 2026, Cohort 8, in partnership with a named Nigerian foundation, with selected participants, IBM-recognised certificates and opportunities for job and internship placement, and an application route through a third-party jobs portal. None of that appears anywhere on IBM's own pages. There is no cohort, there is no selection, there is no scholarship to win and there is no portal to go through. There is a sign-up form on IBM's website. Two things IBM's own pages do not state, so nothing is claimed here about them: whether any examination or certificate fee attaches to a specific credential, and whether a payment card is needed at any point. No device or prerequisite requirement is published either. Separately, and checked the same day, Skill RESET Nigeria, an initiative of the Seyi Makinde Foundation powered by Learnploy, is a genuinely free Nigerian programme whose own apply page says applications closed on 8 November 2026 with learning starting from 16 November, so that one has gone for this cohort.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> The pattern here is worth more than the course. The most reliable way a free thing becomes expensive in Nigeria is an intermediary inserting itself between you and the provider. Take something open, with no deadline and no selection, and repackage it as a scarce, selective, fully funded scholarship with a closing date, and you have manufactured urgency, traffic to a portal, and in the worse cases a form that collects more about you than IBM ever asked for. It is not always malicious. Often it is an aggregator earning from attention. Either way the cost is yours. The test takes thirty seconds and works every time: go to the provider's own domain, find the sign-up, and see whether the restrictions and the deadline you were told about are actually there. If the free thing has no deadline, there is nothing to rush and nobody to route through. Then the opposite caution, because it is the real risk with this one. A catalogue with no deadline and no cohort is extremely easy never to start, and nothing will chase you. The structure that made Skill RESET work for the people who got in is the thing SkillsBuild lacks, so supply it yourself: pick one learning plan, not five, put your own completion date in your calendar tonight, and tell somebody what it is. If a specific credential is the thing you are counting on for a job application, check on IBM's own pages whether an examination fee attaches to it before you build a plan around it, because that is the one question the site does not answer.</p>

## Four, the tool

### QGIS will map anything you can get coordinates for, and it has no paid tier at all

QGIS is desktop mapping and spatial analysis software published by QGIS.org, whose own donation page gives a bank account address in Laax, Switzerland. It is licensed under the GNU General Public License version 2 or later and, in the project's own words, owned by its contributors. The site states that because of its licence QGIS is free and open source software forever, and the download page says the software is and always will be available free of charge if downloaded from QGIS.org. The current release is QGIS 4.2.3, named Belém do Pará, released on 25 September 2026. A long-term release, 3.44.15, named Solothurn, is offered alongside it, and the project advises anyone unsure which to choose to take the long-term release. QGIS 4.4 is in feature freeze.

It runs on Windows, macOS and Linux, with Android and iOS reached through separate companion applications, and only 64-bit Windows builds have been shipped since QGIS 3.20. The capabilities the project groups it under are making maps, including a layout designer and automated atlases and reports, editing layers, with digitising of points, lines, polygons and meshes and construction tools closer to CAD than to a drawing app, processing and analysing, with an analysis toolset and a visual workflow builder, and sharing maps, through web services built on industry standards and publishing to cloud and mobile.

On cost, no paid tier, paid add-on or paid feature is named anywhere on the project's pages. QGIS.org asks for donations and offers a recurring sustaining membership, and it lists commercial support from third-party firms separately, which is a service rather than a licence. The documentation at docs.qgis.org, an installation guide, quick-start tutorials and sample datasets are all published free. Two things the site does not state, so neither does this Brief. It publishes no minimum hardware requirement, so whether it will run acceptably on a low-memory laptop is something you will find out by installing it. And the standalone Windows installers do not include the optional projection grids, which matters only if you are doing precise coordinate transformations. Verified on qgis.org on 12 November 2026.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> Start with the Nigerian half of this. Brief #04 covered the NIPOST digital postcode system, where individual lookups are free, only the shallowest search level is free without an API key, deeper levels need corporate verification and purchased credits, and exact coordinates need special regulatory approval. QGIS is the other side of that coin. It does nothing about getting official address data, and everything about what you do with the spatial data you already hold or can collect yourself with a phone and a form. That is a bigger category than most organisations realise they are sitting on. A programme team can map which communities it actually reached rather than which ones it intended to. A hub can plot where its applicants come from before it writes the next funding proposal. A business can see where its customers are instead of asserting it. A researcher can put health facility or census data on a map and have an argument rather than a table. A map changes a funder's mind in a way a spreadsheet does not, and the main reason Nigerian organisations do not make them is a settled belief that the software costs money, which this one does not and never will. Now the honest part, because this is not a consumer app and pretending otherwise wastes your weekend. It is a professional tool with a professional learning curve, the vocabulary is unfamiliar, projections and coordinate reference systems will confuse you early, and the first hour is genuinely discouraging. Install the long-term release rather than the newest one, work through one official quick-start tutorial end to end before you touch your own data, and start with a single question you want answered on a map rather than exploring the menus.</p>

## Five, the report worth reading

### 86.5 percent of Nigerian adults have a financial account, and 12 percent have used digital credit

NITDA unveiled a Nigeria Tech Outlook Report 2026, with a dashboard, at the Digital Nigeria International Conference and Exhibition in Abuja. It was unveiled by Kashifu Inuwa Abdullahi, Director-General and Chief Executive Officer of the National Information Technology Development Agency. Nairametrics and Voice of Nigeria carried it on 11 November 2026, and Techeconomy and Political Economist on 12 November.

One thing has to be said before any of the figures, because it changes how you should use them. NITDA's own website carries nothing about this report, its dashboard or the event, no copy of the report has been published anywhere this Brief could find, and no address has been given for the dashboard. So every number below is reported from the launch rather than read out of a document. That is a weaker standard than the International Telecommunication Union report in Brief #08, where any reader could open the source and check. Treat these as reported figures, name the outlet if you quote them, and ask NITDA for the report before you build an argument on it.

The evidence base, as reported, is 2,147 households, 539 health facility interviews, 15 key informant interviews and 10 sets of administrative records. Techeconomy adds that the household component was a telephone survey run with the National Bureau of Statistics across the 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory, and quotes the Statistician-General of the Federation, Prince Adeyemi Adeniran. No survey period is published anywhere.

The figures Nairametrics, Voice of Nigeria and Political Economist all carry, in the same terms, are these. 86.5 percent of Nigerian adults hold a financial account. 12 percent accessed digital credit in the preceding twelve months. 40.6 percent personally use a smartphone. 75.9 percent say they would use telemedicine if it were available. 6.6 percent bought goods online in the previous three months. 17.9 percent used a government service online in the preceding twelve months. And 13.1 percent reported experiencing digital financial fraud, with no period stated for that one. Voice of Nigeria alone adds that 11.3 percent have insurance cover, which Political Economist describes as health insurance, that 77.5 percent can perform at least one of eight identified digital tasks, and that the assessment framework runs to 105 indicators, of which 97 had some supporting evidence, 63 fully supported, 34 partially supported and 8 left as data gaps. Techeconomy alone reports that the report is to be annual with a second edition expected by 1 June 2027, and that a broader interactive portal with real-time data exchange with regulators is planned. One figure here should not be repeated at all yet. Techeconomy carries a telemedicine framing that is not compatible with the 75.9 percent willingness figure the other three give, putting more than 80 percent of Nigerians as having used telemedicine at some point and fewer than 20 percent as having actually accessed the service. Those are not the same measure, and until the report is public there is no way to tell which indicator is which.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> 86.5 against 12 is the pair to carry out of this, and it is the number that explains item one of this Brief. Nigeria has very largely solved account ownership and has barely started on credit. The Senate is building a supervisory regime for digital lending at scale in a market where, on these figures, roughly one adult in eight has used digital credit at all. Both of those things can be sensible at once, but anyone writing a fintech strategy on an assumption of saturation should look at the second number again. For product people, 40.6 percent personally using a smartphone is the figure that should govern your decisions, and it is the national version of the argument Brief #08 made with the International Telecommunication Union's continental numbers. Most of the adults you are designing for are reached through somebody else's handset, an agent, a shared device or a feature phone, and a product that assumes a private smartphone is addressing a minority. 6.6 percent buying anything online in three months says the same thing about e-commerce. 17.9 percent using a government service online says the e-government work has barely landed with the public. And 13.1 percent reporting digital financial fraud is the cost of trust showing up as a measurable number, which is worth putting in front of anyone who treats security as a feature to add later. Finally, the practical value. A nationally representative survey with the National Bureau of Statistics behind it is the strongest Nigeria-specific evidence base a grant application or a policy submission can cite, which is exactly why the missing document is not a small complaint. Write to NITDA and ask for it, and until it arrives attribute the figures to the outlet you took them from rather than to the report.</p>
