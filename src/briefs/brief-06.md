## One, the policy change

### The Central Bank is not moving the data deadline, and it is migrating its own

If you work anywhere near payments, the date to write down is 1 January 2027. From that day the Central Bank of Nigeria expects every financial institution and payment service provider operating here to store and manage Nigerian payments transaction data inside Nigeria. The circular setting this out was issued on 15 June 2026, which means the industry has had it for four months and has roughly ten weeks left.

The reason it is news again is that the Bank has just said plainly that the date will not move. Speaking at the CloudReady Nigeria Financial Services Roundtable in Lagos, organised by Africa Hyperscalers, Dr Rakiya Opemi Yusuf, Director of the Payments Systems Supervision Department at the Central Bank of Nigeria, represented by Mr Babatunde Ajiboye, Assistant Director in the same department, said the compliance date stands at 1 January 2027 and that the Bank will begin reviewing compliance from that day. Ajiboye said particular difficulties could be looked at case by case, but that there would be no extension. The detail worth noticing is that the Bank holds some of its own payments data outside the country and has started moving it back, so the regulator is under the same instruction as the regulated.

The argument that has opened up around it is about capacity rather than principle. Writing in the Guardian on 13 October, Abdulrazaq Hamzat, Executive Director of the Foundation for Peace Professionals, welcomed the policy as a step towards digital sovereignty while warning that local hosting means little if the infrastructure, the software and the expertise underneath it remain foreign-controlled, and that moving complex live systems inside the window carries real risk of disruption. The same reporting notes that local storage on its own does nothing about cyberattack or system failure, and that clear technical standards have not been published.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> This is not only a bank problem. If you build, maintain or sell software that touches payment transaction data for a Nigerian institution, your product is now inside somebody's compliance audit, and the question coming at you before Christmas is where your data physically sits. Find out this week, not in December. The second thing to see is the commercial opening. The Bank has in effect created a guaranteed domestic market for data centre capacity, cloud engineering, migration work and security auditing, on a fixed date, and the honest reading of the Guardian piece is that the local industry is not obviously ready to take all of it. That gap is work. If you have infrastructure or security skills, this is the most clearly signposted demand in the Nigerian technology economy right now, and the signpost expires on 1 January.</p>

## Two, the funding move

### Nigeria leads Africa again, and 39 percent of the money belongs to a company that has left

Nigerian startups raised 635 million dollars in the first nine months of 2026, which puts the country back at the top of African startup funding. The figure comes from Africa: The Big Deal, the same tracker Brief #03 used for its continental total, reported by Nairametrics on 21 October. This is a country figure rather than a continental one, so it is not the same measurement as the totals in Brief #01 or Brief #03, and it should not be read against them.

The topline is genuinely good. It is about 2.6 times the 247 million dollars raised in the same nine months of 2025. Seventy-seven Nigerian ventures raised at least 100,000 dollars, up from seventy, the most of any African country. Nigeria took 37 percent of all country-specific funding on the continent, its highest share since 2021. For comparison, over the same period Egypt raised 445 million dollars and was up 10 percent, Kenya raised 201 million and was down 73 percent, and South Africa raised 182 million and was down 58 percent.

Then there is the composition. Four deals account for about 440 million dollars, or roughly 69 percent of the total: Moove at 250 million, Flutterwave at 100 million, Jumia at 50 million and Yellow Card at 40 million. Which leaves about 195 million dollars shared between the other seventy-three companies. And the single largest of those four, Moove's 250 million, is about 39 percent of Nigeria's entire nine-month total, raised by a company that announced on 9 October that it is ending its Nigerian operations and handing drivers roughly 35 billion naira worth of vehicles on the way out. Sector-wise, logistics and transport raised 780 million dollars across the continent and overtook fintech at 760 million, largely on the strength of Moove and Spiro.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> Read past the headline before you take it as a signal about your own chances. Strip out the four big rounds and the picture for an ordinary Nigerian company raising its first or second cheque is about 195 million dollars spread across seventy-three businesses, which averages well under three million each. That is the market you are actually in, and it is a far more useful planning number than 635 million. The deal count is the figure to take heart from. Seventy-seven companies clearing 100,000 dollars, up from seventy, says more small cheques are being written even as the big ones distort the total. The Moove line is the one to sit with, though. The largest single vote of confidence in Nigerian technology this year was cast by investors in a company that has since decided Nigeria is not where it will operate. Capital raised in Lagos and deployed elsewhere still counts in the table and does not count on the ground, and if you are using these tables to argue the ecosystem is healthy, somebody in the room will know that.</p>

## Three, the free course

### Carnegie Mellon is running a cost-free AI certificate for Africa, and it closes on 15 November

Carnegie Mellon University Africa is taking applications for its Certificate in Artificial Intelligence for Africa. The university's own page says the programme is cost-free for qualified applicants. Applications are open from 15 October to 15 November 2026, and the cohort starts on 17 January 2027.

It is six courses, delivered online, with hands-on exercises using industry-standard tools. Two of them sit in a perspective and context track, Artificial Intelligence: Past, Present and Future, and AI and Machine Learning in Africa. The other four are technical: Python for AI, Mathematical Foundations of Machine Learning, Machine Learning Essentials, and Applied Generative AI. Each course runs to fourteen or fifteen lessons of about three hours, roughly forty-five hours a course, and the university advises planning at least five to six hours a week. You earn a digital badge for each course and a shareable, verifiable certificate once all six are done. It is not for university credit, and the page says so rather than letting the name imply it.

The entry requirements are real and are worth reading before you apply. You need introductory coursework in linear algebra, introductory knowledge of probability and statistics, and prior computer programming experience, plus a laptop or desktop and a reliable internet connection. Enrolment is not automatic, places in the cohort are limited, and applicants are selected from what they submit.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> Most free AI training aimed at this continent is a few hours long and ends in a badge. This is six courses and something close to two hundred and seventy hours of work, from a university whose engineering school people have actually heard of, at no fee. That combination is rare enough to be worth rearranging a month for. Be honest with yourself about the prerequisites first, because they are the filter: if you have never written code and have not touched linear algebra, this cohort is not the one, and the useful move is to spend the next eight weeks fixing that and apply to the next. If you do clear them, the deadline is the constraint rather than the content. Applications close on 15 November, a CV and a short statement of professional goals are part of it, and a statement written in a hurry on the last evening reads exactly like one.</p>

## Four, the tool

### Kiwix puts the whole of Wikipedia on your phone, and then you can switch the internet off

Kiwix is free, open-source software from a Swiss non-profit, the Kiwix Association in Lausanne, and what it does is simple enough to describe in one line. It downloads enormous libraries of free reference material, starting with the whole of Wikipedia, packs them into a single file, and lets you read and search all of it on a phone, a laptop or a shared computer with no internet connection whatsoever. Its own tagline is that when there is no internet, there is Kiwix. The organisation reports more than ten million users across more than two hundred and twelve countries and territories, in over a hundred languages.

The catalogue goes well past Wikipedia. There are offline builds of Wiktionary, Project Gutenberg, Stack Exchange, medical references, TED talks and a long list of school curricula. You download the library you want once, over whatever connection you can get, and after that it costs nothing to use and consumes no data at all. There is also a Hotspot product, which turns a small device into a local server so that a classroom or an office full of phones can read the same library over local wifi with nothing reaching the internet.

One honest note on cost. The software and the content libraries are free, and the organisation says most of its revenue comes from donations and that it shows no advertisements and collects no personal data. Some of the physical hardware solutions are sold rather than given away, so if you go looking at the Hotspot devices, expect a price there.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> Everyone reading this has had the week where the data ran out at the wrong moment or the network dropped in the middle of something that mattered. The quiet trick here is that reference material does not need to be live. Wikipedia from last month answers almost every question you were going to ask it today, and a copy sitting on your own device answers it during a power cut, on a bus, in a village with one bar of signal, and for nothing. If you teach, run a training programme, support a community centre or have children who are told to go and research something, this is the highest-value free download available to you, and the Hotspot idea in particular turns one prepared device into a library for a whole room. Set it up on the connection you have now rather than the one you will wish you had later. That is the entire discipline it asks of you.</p>

## Five, the report worth reading

### Nigerians are enrolling in AI courses twice as fast, and still rank 81st at putting them to work

Coursera published the eighth edition of its Global Skills Report on 19 October. It draws on a subset of learning data from more than three hundred million learners across Coursera and Udemy, and this edition pairs that with national readiness data from Oxford Insights' Government AI Readiness Index to build something new, an AI-Human Skills Synergy Index covering ninety-eight countries.

Nigeria's learning numbers are the striking part. Enrolments in artificial intelligence micro-credentials rose 93 percent year on year, against a 33 percent rise in micro-credential enrolments overall, so AI learning is growing nearly three times as fast as everything else. Underneath that, AI security enrolments were up 137 percent, generative AI up 126 percent, agentic workflows up 95 percent and AI courses generally up 28 percent. The one that is easy to miss is critical thinking, up 113 percent, with complex problem-solving up 59 percent and innovation up 6 percent.

The index itself is less flattering, and that is the point of it. Nigeria ranks 81st of the ninety-eight countries measured, and fifth of the eleven Sub-Saharan African countries covered. The index is not a measure of how much AI learning is happening. It measures how far learners build AI skills alongside the human skills needed to apply them, adjusted for the country's broader readiness to absorb either. Kais Zribi, General Manager for the Middle East and Africa at Coursera, framed the next step as connecting AI learning to actual employment and entrepreneurship rather than simply widening access to courses.

<p class="why"><b>Why it matters to you.</b> Put the two halves together and the report is saying something uncomfortable and useful. Nigerians are learning AI faster than almost anyone, and the country still sits in the bottom fifth on turning that learning into applied capability. The gap is not enthusiasm and it is not access. It is what happens after the certificate, which is a readiness problem, an employment problem and a how-you-study problem all at once. The individual lesson is the one buried in the critical thinking figure. A 113 percent rise in people studying how to think alongside a 93 percent rise in people studying AI is the healthier pattern, because the AI credential on its own is now a commodity and the judgement to use it is not. The practical version, if you are learning this year: for every course you take on a tool, take one on the thing the tool is for. Write something, cost something, argue a case, run the analysis and then explain to somebody why it is wrong. That is the half the index says we are short of, and it is the half that does not go out of date when the model version changes.</p>
